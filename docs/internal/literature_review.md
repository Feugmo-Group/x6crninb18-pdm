## Thread 1 — Point Defect Model (PDM) lineage and its application to stainless steels

The PDM originates with Macdonald and coworkers in the early 1980s and has gone through three generations (PDM-I/II/III), each extending how vacancy generation/annihilation and film-growth/dissolution are treated at the metal/film and film/solution interfaces.

**Key works:**

1. Macdonald, D.D. (1992). "The Point Defect Model for the Passive State." *Journal of the Electrochemical Society*, 139(12), 3434–3449. — Foundational statement of the PDM: passive film treated as a bipolar semiconductor with point-defect (cation/oxygen vacancy, metal interstitial) transport under a constant electric field, giving a logarithmic-to-inverse-logarithmic film growth law and an analytic breakdown/pitting criterion. This is the paper essentially every later PDM application cites as the starting point.
2. Macdonald, D.D. (2011). "The history of the Point Defect Model for the passive state: A brief review of film growth aspects." *Electrochimica Acta*, 56(4), 1761–1772. — Retrospective review tracing PDM-I through PDM-III, summarizing successes (steady-state thickness laws, breakdown criteria) and open issues (bilayer structure, restructuring, alloy composition effects).
3. Bojinov, M. et al. "A Mixed-Conduction Model for the Oxidation of Stainless Steel in a High-Temperature Electrolyte: Estimation of Kinetic Parameters of Oxide Layer Growth and Restructuring." *J. Electrochem. Soc.* — The Mixed-Conduction Model (MCM), conceptually a sibling framework to PDM, applied specifically to stainless steel oxide growth with EIS-fitted kinetic/transport parameters; representative of the "fit PDM/MCM-family kinetics to impedance data" tradition that dominates the field.
4. Applications to specific grades: "Study on the passivation properties of austenitic stainless steel 316LN based on the point defect model" (*Corrosion Science*, 2024) and "Passivity of AISI 321 stainless steel in 0.5 M H2SO4 studied by Mott–Schottky analysis in conjunction with the point defect model" (*Arabian J. Chem.*) — recent (2023-2024) examples showing PDM is still actively fit to specific austenitic grades, almost always via Mott–Schottky/EIS in room-temperature or mildly acidic electrolytes, not high-temperature reactor water.
5. "Adaptation of the Point Defect Model to Simulate Oxidation Kinetics of 316L Stainless Steel in the Pressurised Water Reactor Environment" — the closest prior example of PDM applied to a PWR-relevant austenitic grade in high-temperature water; parameters obtained by conventional least-squares/EIS fitting, not from post-mortem EDX/SIMS depth profiles and not from a PINN inversion.

**Relation to our paper:** Our PDM formulation is a direct descendant of this lineage (via Li et al. 2020, see Thread 2), but the vast majority of PDM-to-stainless-steel applications (points 3–5) are calibrated against in-situ electrochemical (EIS/Mott-Schottky) data in bench-scale electrolytes, not against ex-situ compositional depth profiles from real BWR-representative high-temperature-water autoclave exposures. We are not aware of a prior PDM parameterization for X6CrNiNb18-10/AISI 347 in true BWR HTW conditions using EDX/SIMS depth data as the inversion target — this is the specific empirical gap contribution (1) fills.

## Thread 2 — Li et al. 2020 SCW_PDM and follow-on work

**Key work:**

1. Li, Y., Macdonald, D.D., Yang, J., Qiu, J., Wang, S. (2020). "Point defect model for the corrosion of steels in supercritical water: Part I, film growth kinetics." *Corrosion Science*, 163, 108280. DOI via ScienceDirect (S0010938X19309722). — Develops SCW_PDM: the barrier oxide layer grows into the metal via oxygen-vacancy production at the metal/barrier interface and annihilation at the barrier/outer-layer interface, with the barrier/outer interface remaining pinned near the original metal surface (Pilling–Bedworth-consistent moving-boundary picture). Provides atomistic rate laws connecting interfacial reaction rate constants to barrier-layer and total-scale thickness vs. time, explicitly for supercritical water (SCW, ~500°C/25 MPa) conditions on ferritic/martensitic and austenitic steels.
2. Related/companion papers in the same programme: "Estimation of kinetic parameters of the corrosion layer constituents on steels in supercritical water coolant conditions" (*Corrosion Science*) and "Fundamental atomic-scale kinetic models for the oxidation of iron/nickel-based alloys in low-density supercritical water" (2023) — extend the same rate-law machinery to atomistic/DFT-informed rate constants and to Fe/Ni-based alloys, showing the SCW_PDM lineage remains active through 2023.
3. "Electrochemical techniques and mechanisms for the corrosion of metals and alloys in sub- and supercritical aqueous systems" (OSTI/ScienceDirect, recent) — a broader review situating SCW_PDM among other SCW corrosion-mechanism approaches.

**Relation to our paper:** Our governing equations are a direct BWR-HTW adaptation of the Li et al. (2020) SCW_PDM moving-boundary formalism (240°C/7 MPa subcritical HTW rather than SCW conditions, austenitic AISI 347 rather than the ferritic-martensitic/generic steels emphasized in the original SCW work). No SCW_PDM follow-on paper we found (a) targets subcritical BWR HTW conditions, (b) targets this specific Nb-stabilized alloy, or (c) performs an inverse fit to real depth-profile data via any method, let alone a PINN — all prior SCW_PDM work either forward-simulates or fits via conventional (non-neural, non-identifiability-aware) least squares to weight-gain/thickness data.

## Thread 3 — Veile et al. 2024 and AISI 347 / Nb-stabilized steel oxide growth in BWR/PWR water

**Key work:**

1. Veile, G., Hirpara, R., Lackmann, S., Weihe, S. (2024). "Investigation of Oxide Layer Development of X6CrNiNb18-10 Stainless Steel Exposed to High-Temperature Water." *Materials*, 17(18), 4500. DOI: 10.3390/ma17184500. — Exposed X6CrNiNb18-10 (17.6 wt% Cr, 10.6 wt% Ni, 0.62 wt% Nb) coupons to simulated BWR HTW (240 ± 0.54°C, 7 ± 0.07 MPa, 0.4 ± 0.011 ppm dissolved O2, 0.055 µS/cm conductivity) for 72, 168, and 480 h; characterized cross-sections by FIB-lift-out TEM/EDX line scans plus SEM and XPS. Found a dual-layer oxide (Cr-rich protective primary layer, Fe-rich non-protective secondary layer) with the primary (Cr) layer following an approximately parabolic power law (fitted k = 6.521 nm, n = 0.4964) and the secondary (Fe) layer following a near-cubic law (k = 64.51 nm, n = 0.2209); Ni content in the layers stayed roughly constant until a sufficiently developed Fe-rich secondary layer allowed Ni enrichment there. First report of Nb2O5 detected in a BWR HTW environment, and explicitly stated as the first detailed characterization of AISI 347 primary-oxide development under BWR conditions.
2. "Influence of high temperature water on AISI 304, AISI 347 and ER 347 regarding their threshold value and surface oxidation" (companion/related conference or journal piece, same research group) — comparative look at 304 vs. 347 base metal and weld filler (ER 347) under similar HTW exposure, situating the alloy choice within a broader Cr-Ni-Nb stabilized-steel comparison.
3. "Influence of dissolved oxygen concentration on the oxide film formed on 304 stainless steel in high temperature water" and "Influence of Mechanical Surface Treatments on Oxide Properties Formed on 304L Stainless Steel in Simulated BWR and PWR Primary Water" — establish that dissolved-O2 level and surface treatment materially change oxide film character on the more commonly studied AISI 304/304L, providing the comparison baseline for why 347 (Nb-stabilized, used for weld-decay resistance) needed its own characterization.

**Relation to our paper:** Veile et al. (2024) supplies exactly the three-time-point EDX/SIMS depth-profile dataset our PDM inversion is fit to, and explicitly notes theirs is the first oxide-layer characterization of this alloy in BWR HTW — but their own analysis stops at empirical power-law fits (L = k·t^n) to each element layer separately; they do not attempt a mechanistic (vacancy-transport) model, do not connect Cr/Fe/Ni layer kinetics through a shared physical parameter set, and (by construction, with only 3 time points) cannot assess whether their own fitted exponents/rate constants are meaningfully constrained by the data. Our paper is the first to (a) fit a mechanistic PDM to this dataset, (b) show where that mechanistic fit agrees/disagrees with their empirical power-law description and how the two extrapolate differently at long times, and (c) formally characterize the parameter identifiability that a 3-point dataset permits — a question Veile et al. do not raise.

## Thread 4 — BWR/PWR water-chemistry corrosion literature (HWC/NWC, IGSCC relevance, current predictive tools)

**Key works:**

1. Multiple ScienceDirect/EPRI-adjacent literature on Hydrogen Water Chemistry (HWC) for BWRs — establishes that under Normal Water Chemistry (NWC), dissolved O2 is ~200–400 ppb and drives oxidation/IGSCC susceptibility of internals, while HWC injection lowers electrochemical corrosion potential (ECP) below the ~ -230 mV(SHE) IGSCC-protection criterion by suppressing dissolved O2 (target commonly <20 ppb), at the cost of changing oxide film character from thick/loose hematite-type to thin/dense magnetite-type. This is the operational, empirical/ECP-criterion-based framework that plant chemistry programs (and EPRI guidance) actually use — it is a threshold/criterion approach, not a predictive mechanistic film-growth or crack-growth-rate model tied to a first-principles kinetic parameter set.
2. Bojinov, M. et al., mixed-conduction-model EIS studies of stainless steel oxidation kinetics in high-temperature electrolyte — the closest mechanistic-kinetic tradition actually used by the nuclear-materials community for oxide film growth, but calibrated to in-situ EIS in autoclave loops, not to post-mortem EDX/SIMS depth data, and not extended to long-time extrapolation or formal identifiability treatment.
3. "Corrosion of Stainless Steel in Simulated Nuclear Reactor Primary Coolant—Experiments and Modeling" (*Materials*, 17(5), 1148, 2024) — recent (2024) example combining experiment and modeling for stainless steel in simulated PWR primary coolant, indicating active current interest in mechanistic film-growth modeling for LWR-relevant conditions.
4. Machine learning for modeling intergranular stress corrosion cracking (IGSCC) of stainless steels in light water reactors with uncertainty quantification and explainability, *npj Materials Degradation* (2025). — A directly venue-relevant (npj Materials Degradation) precedent: uses CatBoost/NGBoost/Gaussian-process/TabNet models on a curated crack-growth-rate database across BWR/PWR conditions, explicitly reporting calibrated uncertainty and explainability, illustrating that the nuclear-materials-degradation community (and this specific target journal) already expects and rewards UQ-aware, ML-adjacent modeling — but this line of work is purely data-driven (ML on crack growth rate, not on oxide-film mechanistic kinetics) and does not touch PDM or film growth at all.

**Relation to our paper:** The nuclear-materials community's working models for LWR internals corrosion/IGSCC risk are either (a) empirical ECP/dissolved-O2 threshold criteria (HWC/NWC guidance, not mechanistic), (b) EIS-fitted MCM/PDM kinetics calibrated in bench autoclaves rather than to depth-profile data from realistic exposure coupons, or (c) purely data-driven ML models of crack growth rate with no connection to oxide chemistry/thickness evolution. Our paper offers a mechanistic, parameter-identifiable, long-time-extrapolable oxide-growth model calibrated directly to compositional depth-profile data of the type routinely generated in surveillance/qualification programs — filling the gap between threshold-only guidance and pure black-box ML, while meeting the UQ/explainability bar the npj Materials Degradation venue has already established as its norm (item 4 above).

## Thread 5 — PINNs applied to corrosion/electrochemistry/degradation, and PINN-based PDM fitting specifically

**Critical finding:** there is already a directly related prior work from (evidently) the same research group.

1. Farooqi, M., Bösing, I., Tetsassi Feugmo, C.G. (2025). "A physics-informed neural network approach to the point defect model for electrochemical oxide film growth." arXiv:2510.02872. — Applies PINNs to solve and invert the (generic, halide-free-electrolyte) PDM — a stiff, coupled Nernst–Planck/Poisson/Butler–Volmer system on a moving boundary. Identifies and addresses four PINN failure modes: (1) imbalanced loss components across physical processes, (2) numerical instabilities from variable-scale disparity, (3) difficulty enforcing boundary conditions in multiphysics systems, and (4) convergence to mathematically valid but physically meaningless solutions. Uses nondimensionalization, NTK-based adaptive loss balancing, robust BC enforcement, and hybrid training to reach sub-1% relative error against finite-element benchmarks. Uses synthetic/FEM-benchmark data, not real alloy/EDX data; does not target a specific alloy/environment; does not perform identifiability analysis (profile likelihood/bootstrap); does not discuss the soft-mode-vs-hard-mode joint field+parameter inversion failure mode our paper documents; no long-time extrapolation or operating-envelope sensitivity study.
2. Broader PINN-for-electrochemistry precedents, mostly in batteries rather than corrosion: "A physics-informed neural network approach to parameter estimation of lithium-ion battery electrochemical model" (*J. Power Sources* 2024) and "Physics-informed neural networks for degradation diagnosis of lithium-ion batteries via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy" (2025) — show PINN parameter-identification methodology is established for battery P2D-type electrochemical models, but not yet for corrosion/PDM applications at the field+real-data level.
3. "PINNverse: Accurate parameter estimation in differential equations from noisy data with constrained physics-informed neural networks" (arXiv:2504.05248, 2025) — a general (non-corrosion) diagnosis of exactly the tension our contribution (3) documents: standard soft-penalty PINN loss balancing between data-fit and physics-residual terms causes noise-overfitting at the expense of physical accuracy on sparse/noisy inverse problems, and proposes a constrained-optimization reformulation as a fix. This is strong independent, contemporaneous (2025) corroboration — from a completely different application domain — that the soft-mode failure mode we document and the need for a harder physics-enforcement mechanism (our differentiable RK4 hard-mode fix) is a real, recognized, and currently active research problem in the PINN inverse-problem literature generally, not an idiosyncrasy of our system.
4. Corrosion-adjacent but not PDM: "Physics-Augmented Neural Network for Predicting Corrosion Inhibition Efficiency of Green Inhibitors" — PINN/physics-augmented NN work in corrosion, but targeting inhibitor efficiency prediction (a regression/property-prediction task), not mechanistic film-growth-kinetics inversion.

**Relation to our paper:** This thread contains the single most important framing fact for the Introduction: a PINN-PDM methodology paper already exists from adjacent authors (Farooqi, Bösing, Tetsassi Feugmo 2025, arXiv:2510.02872), establishing that PINNs *can* solve/invert the generic PDM system against synthetic/FEM benchmarks and cataloguing four training-level failure modes. Our paper is the natural, non-redundant next step: (a) real alloy, real environment, real EDX/SIMS data rather than synthetic/FEM benchmarks; (b) a fifth failure mode specific to the *inverse* (joint field+parameter) setting under *sparse real data* — soft-mode inversion satisfying the data loss while leaving physically meaningful residual slack — which is a different failure class from the four forward/training-stability issues catalogued in 2510.02872; (c) a hard-mode differentiable-RK4 fix and a proposed self-consistency-check reporting standard, going beyond diagnosis to a concrete remedy and a community reporting norm; (d) identifiability analysis, which 2510.02872 does not attempt; (e) long-time extrapolation and operating-envelope sensitivity, which are absent from 2510.02872. This should be framed explicitly as building on / completing the applied half of that companion methodology paper, not as a from-scratch claim that "no one has tried PINN-PDM before."

## Thread 6 — Parameter identifiability in electrochemical/corrosion kinetic models generally

**Key works:**

1. "Identifiability and distinguishability concepts in electrochemistry" (*Automatica*/related, 1996) — an old but foundational statement that structural (non-)identifiability is a real concern in electrochemical model fitting, predating modern profile-likelihood tooling.
2. "Practical identifiability of electrochemical P2D models for lithium-ion batteries" (*J. Applied Electrochemistry*, 2021/KIT repository) — a modern, rigorous practical-identifiability study (the closest methodological analogue to what we do) applied to battery pseudo-2D models: distinguishes structural from practical identifiability and shows several nominally-identifiable P2D parameters are not practically constrained by typical experimental protocols.
3. "Parameter identifiability analysis and visualization in large-scale kinetic models of biosystems" (PMC, systems biology) and general profile-likelihood methodology references — establish profile-likelihood-based identifiability analysis (holding a parameter fixed across a grid, re-optimizing nuisance parameters, and reading off confidence bounds from the likelihood profile) as a mature, standard tool in systems biology and increasingly in battery electrochemistry, but not (based on this search) in the PDM/corrosion-kinetics literature specifically.
4. Absence of hits: no paper surfaced applying profile-likelihood or bootstrap identifiability analysis to PDM, MCM, or any other corrosion/passive-film kinetic model fit to sparse experimental (EIS, weight-gain, or depth-profile) data. All PDM/MCM parameter-estimation papers found in Threads 1, 2, 4 (Bojinov et al., Li et al., the 316L/316LN/321 passivation papers) report best-fit parameter values, sometimes with least-squares confidence intervals or genetic-algorithm-derived fit quality, but none report a systematic identifiability analysis (profile likelihood, bootstrap resampling, or Fisher-information-based practical-identifiability classification) of which kinetic parameters are and are not constrained by the data actually available.

**Relation to our paper:** Contribution (2) — explicit profile-likelihood + bootstrap identifiability analysis showing which of the 5 PDM kinetic parameters are/aren't constrained by 3 exposure-time points — is genuinely rare in the corrosion/PDM literature specifically, even though the *method* (profile likelihood identifiability) is well-established and routine in adjacent fields (systems biology, and now battery P2D modeling, per item 2). The correct framing is: this is a known, mature statistical technique that the PDM/corrosion-kinetics community has essentially never applied to itself, despite the field routinely fitting 4-6-parameter mechanistic models to 3-5 data points — exactly the identifiability-starved regime the technique was built to diagnose. This is an import of established methodology into an under-served subfield, not the invention of a new identifiability method.

## Synthesis: the sharpest gap statement(s)

**Single sharpest, unifying gap statement (works for both audiences):** *Point Defect Model kinetics for stainless-steel oxide growth have, for four decades, been fit to sparse experimental data (3-6 points is typical, whether from EIS spectra or depth profiles) without ever asking whether that data actually constrains the 4-6 parameters being estimated — and the one prior attempt to accelerate PDM inversion with physics-informed neural networks (Farooqi, Bösing & Tetsassi Feugmo 2025) validated the forward/inverse machinery only on synthetic benchmarks, leaving open both the real-data identifiability question and a distinct sparse-real-data inversion failure mode (soft-mode joint field+parameter fits that satisfy the data loss while leaving the physics residual slack) that only appears once real, noisy, few-point depth-profile data is used as the inversion target.* This paper closes both gaps simultaneously, using AISI 347 in BWR HTW as the first alloy/environment where the full pipeline — real EDX/SIMS data, PDM inversion, identifiability audit, and long-time/operating-envelope extrapolation — has been carried out together.

This single statement is strong because it braids three otherwise-separate observations that no single existing paper combines: (i) PDM fitting has never been identifiability-audited (Thread 6); (ii) the sibling PINN-PDM paper stopped at synthetic validation (Thread 5); (iii) AISI 347 in BWR HTW had no mechanistic film-growth model at all before Veile et al.'s purely empirical power-law description (Thread 3).

**(a) Framing for the nuclear/BWR materials community (npj Materials Degradation, primary target):** Lead with the *material and application* gap, not the ML method. Current predictive practice for LWR internals oxide growth is either an operational ECP/dissolved-O2 threshold criterion (HWC/NWC guidance) with no mechanistic film-thickness prediction, or purely data-driven ML models of crack growth rate (cf. the 2025 npj Materials Degradation IGSCC-UQ paper) that say nothing about oxide chemistry or thickness evolution. AISI 347 — chosen precisely for weld-decay resistance and used in BWR internals/piping — had *no* mechanistic oxide-growth model prior to this work; Veile et al. (2024) established the first EDX/SIMS characterization but stopped at per-element empirical power laws with no shared physical parameter set and no extrapolation validity check. This paper supplies the first mechanistic, uncertainty-quantified oxide-growth model for this alloy/environment, demonstrates that its 3.5x long-time divergence from naive power-law extrapolation is directly consequential for structural-material life prediction, and shows model robustness across the BWR operating envelope (temperature, dissolved O2) — directly actionable for surveillance-program design and HWC/NWC chemistry-control decisions. The identifiability finding is framed as a warning to the field: sparse surveillance-style datasets (2-4 exposure times is typical for practical reasons) cannot constrain full multi-parameter PDM fits without saying which parameters are safe to report point-estimates for.

**(b) Framing for the electrochemistry/PDM community (Corrosion Science fallback):** Lead with the *methodological rigor* gap. Forty years of PDM parameter estimation (Macdonald 1992 onward, through Li et al. 2020's SCW_PDM and the 316L/316LN/321 literature) has never been paired with a formal identifiability analysis, despite chronically fitting 4-6 kinetic parameters to handfuls of data points — exactly the regime in which profile-likelihood/bootstrap methods (mature in systems biology and now battery P2D modeling, per the KIT 2021 practical-identifiability paper) are indispensable. Simultaneously, the one existing PINN-PDM paper (arXiv:2510.02872) demonstrated forward/inverse PINN machinery only against synthetic FEM benchmarks and catalogued training-stability failure modes, not the sparse-real-data soft-mode inversion failure this paper documents and fixes with a differentiable hard-mode RK4 integrator. This paper is offered as a methodological template — self-consistency-check reporting standard plus identifiability audit — that the PDM community (and PINN-for-electrochemistry practitioners generally) should adopt whenever fitting mechanistic film-growth models to necessarily-sparse real exposure data, illustrated end-to-end on a genuinely novel alloy/environment (AISI 347, BWR HTW) with a companion spatial (Tier-2) validation against independent EDX composition-depth data as a zero-parameter cross-check.

Both framings share the same evidentiary backbone (Threads 1-6 above) and can be interleaved in a single Introduction: open with the material/application motivation (a), pivot to the methodological gap (b) as the mechanism by which the paper delivers a trustworthy answer, and close by naming both the empirical antecedent (Veile et al. 2024) and the methodological antecedent (Farooqi/Bösing/Tetsassi Feugmo 2025) as the two papers this work directly completes.
